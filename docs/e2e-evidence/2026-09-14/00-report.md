## The case study's questions, answered with measurements

**"List products — filtering by category, pagination, and sorting by effective
price."** All three live. Sorting is by the _effective_ price: a 60 %-discounted
200.00 product sorts ahead of a 100.00 one, because the shopper pays less for it.

**"Get single product details (the most highly trafficked endpoint)."**
**4 375 req/s at p50 22 ms and p99 35 ms**, 100 connections — the fastest surface in
the system, as it should be. It is one hash read where the listing is a sorted-set
range plus a multi-get.

**"Create, cancel, and assign promotions."** All three, with cancel idempotent and
a missing promotion answering `404`.

**"A product can have at most one active promotion at a time."** Enforced per
target at write time by a GiST exclusion constraint — **eight concurrent writers on
one product produce one `201` and seven `409`** — and resolved at read time, where a
product's own 70 % beats a category's 50 % because the shopper gets the better of
the two.

### Scenario A — 500 000 rows under serverless constraints

|                            |                                                                          |
| -------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| `POST /api/vendor/imports` | `202` in **834 ms**, including streaming and hashing 22 MB               |
| Chunks                     | 6, each on its first attempt                                             |
| Rows                       | 500 000 processed, **0 rejected**                                        |
| Worker memory              | **flat at 51.6-54.9 MiB** across all six chunks, against a 256 MiB limit |

**Flat is the property, not the peak** — a file six times larger draws the same
line, because what is resident is one batch rather than one file.

**Killed mid-import with `SIGKILL`**, the chunk resumed from its checkpoint: the
killed chunk returned **87 783 rows, not 1 000 + 87 783**, and the six chunks summed
to **exactly 500 000**. The arithmetic is the proof; it would have read 501 000 the
other way.

**The memory floor is between 32 and 48 MiB.** A 48 MiB container with a 32 MiB
heap completed a 100 000-row import in 11 s; a 32 MiB container was OOM-killed at
startup before claiming anything. **What fails first is the Node runtime's own
footprint, not the batch** — the import path runs in under a quarter of what the
case study allows. And it failed _safely_: nothing stored, nothing reported as
success, and a normally sized worker later completed the same import unprompted.

### Scenario B — a flash sale on 100 000 products

Twice the case study's 50 000.

**The measurement that could have falsified the design, reported first:** a
read-only burst of **34 400 requests moved PostgreSQL by ten transactions** — and
those ten are the readiness probe and the workers. The idle stack commits about one
a second on its own. **The read path does not reach PostgreSQL.** Had that delta
tracked the request count, the read model would have been a cache with a fallback.

|                             | req/s                | p50   | p99    | non-2xx | errors |
| --------------------------- | -------------------- | ----- | ------ | ------- | ------ |
| quiet                       | 1 721                | 28 ms | 42 ms  | 0       | 0      |
| during the sale's recompute | 1 280                | 32 ms | 104 ms | **0**   | **0**  |
| during a 500 000-row import | 3 614 (detail route) | 26 ms | 51 ms  | **0**   | **0**  |

Propagation from the `201`: **first product discounted at 915 ms, all 100 000 at
12.2 s**; cancel reverts the first at 993 ms and all 100 000 at 10.6 s.

**A new product added to the category mid-sale is served at the sale price 444 ms
later**, with no further request — the case study's own sentence, through the real
queues.

So the bottleneck question, answered as a measurement: **the writes never block the
reads, because the reads never reach the store the writes contend for.** A sale
costs a quarter to two fifths of the throughput and doubles to triples the tail
while it runs; an import costs about a sixth and 16 ms. Both are felt as latency,
neither as failure.

## What broke, and what it cost

**One defect, found by restarting PostgreSQL under load and fixed the same night
(#128).** The recompute survived — 100 001 of 100 001 came back — but the api
process died, because `createPool` attached no `error` listener and node-postgres
documents that an idle client error with no listener is fatal. **A database restart
took the HTTP listener with it, so storefront reads that never touch PostgreSQL
failed for its duration** — the design's central claim failing in the one place it
can, through an unhandled event rather than a query.

**Redis restarted mid-recompute is a pass**: 13 × `503` across about six and a half
seconds, no stale price, no fallback, everything correct afterwards. The
reconciler's five-minute chain survived the same restart and its next sweep fired on
schedule.

## Known gaps, named here rather than left to be found

1. **A lost announcement is repaired after the watermark reaches it, not within one
   reconciler period.** The sweep advances its watermark at most an hour per run,
   so on a database whose watermark is behind, repair latency is the catch-up time.
   Measured: a destroyed announcement was repaired about fifteen minutes later, and
   only because that promotion's own `starts_at` fell in the window being caught up
   through.
2. **A chunk that exhausts its attempts stays `running` with an expired lease** and
   nothing reclaims it; the vendor is then refused by the one-import-per-vendor
   index. The orphan-chunk sweep that would reclaim it does not exist.
3. **A duplicate SKU inside one file is counted in neither `rowsProcessed` nor
   `rowsRejected`**, so the vendor's arithmetic does not close. `ON CONFLICT` cannot
   affect the same row twice, so the dedupe is forced by the mechanism; a third
   counter is the honest fix, since a row that lost to a later row was not rejected.
4. **One vendor's import silently replaces another's product** for the same SKU, and
   **a third party can permanently lock a vendor out** by registering their file
   first. Both are the missing vendor column; ADR-0005 records
   `unique (vendor, file_sha256)` as the fix and names the scope as the owner's to
   set.
5. **Nothing authenticates any route.** `vendor` is a form field, and
   `GET /api/vendor/imports/:id` returns any import by id.

## How to read the numbers

Every file ends with a **Vantage** section. That is not ceremony: the day's most
repeated defect was a figure that answered a narrower question than the sentence
above it, and two of them were caught in this campaign — a schema comparison whose
query errored on both sides and reported "identical", and a transaction delta that
came back **negative** because the counters had been reset underneath it.

- **Memory is container accounting**, never host RSS. The two are different
  quantities and a host sampler cannot see a container boundary.
- **Latencies are loopback**, with no network between client and server, so they
  are a ceiling for this machine rather than a service level.
- **Durations name their system.** The 29 s on record for a 500 000-row import is
  the importer alone with nothing consuming; 128 s is the whole system keeping its
  read model current. Neither may be quoted without saying which.
- Where two runs of one thing disagree — 1 280 against 1 013 req/s under a sale —
  both are reported. Picking one would be the more confident and less honest report.
